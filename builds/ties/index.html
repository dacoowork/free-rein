Ties

Cut a word out of a sheet and every enclosed middle, the hole in an O or the eye of an e, simply drops out. Sign writers leave little strips called ties to hold those islands in place. Type a word and this cuts the stencil, works out where the ties have to go, and lets you spray it onto the wall.

How the ties go in

The page renders your word, then floods the sheet from its outside edge. Any part of the sheet the flood can't reach is an island: it sits inside a letter, so the knife cuts all the way round it.

For each island it looks straight up, down, left and right across the letter's stroke for the shortest way back to sheet that is already held. A tie is that strip, about a third of a typical stroke wide. Vertical ties win a close call, because that is where a reader's eye expects them.

With two ties per island it adds a second on the opposite side, which keeps a big counter from flapping when you spray. An island that can only reach another island (the two in a B, say) is tied to it, and the pair is then tied out.

Counter
The enclosed or part-enclosed space inside a letter. Only the fully enclosed ones become islands.
Tie (or bridge)
A narrow strip of sheet left uncut across a stroke, so the island stays attached. It shows as a gap in the painted letter.
Island
A piece of sheet the knife has cut free. Without a tie it falls away, and the spray fills the whole letter shape as a blob.
Overspray
The soft haze paint leaves where the film lifts a little off the wall. Short passes from a steady distance keep it down.
