01 · THE FLAT LINE

On an ordinary scale, nothing happens for forty years

Draw the counts on a plain axis and every chip before about 2012 sits on the floor. The Pentium 4's 42 million transistors are a rounding error next to 208 billion. That is what exponential growth looks like from the end: all the action seems recent, because each doubling adds more than all the doublings before it put together.

02 · THE RULER

On a log scale, it's nearly a straight line

Give every factor of ten the same height and the cloud turns into a ruler. A straight line on this scale means a fixed doubling time. Fit one through the single chips and it comes out at a little over two years per doubling, held for five decades by thousands of engineers who planned around the line itself.

03 · TWO GUESSES

Gordon Moore guessed twice

In 1965 Moore, then at Fairchild, noticed the number of components on a chip doubling every year and guessed it would carry on for ten years. In 1975 he slowed his guess to every two years. Start both lines at the 4004 and the first one is off by a factor of millions by 2024; the second runs straight through the cloud.

04 · BIGGER, NOT JUST SMALLER

The recent climb is partly about size

Shrinking transistors did most of the work, but not all of it. The M1 Ultra is two M1 Max dies joined edge to edge; the B200 is two dies in one package. Cerebras stopped cutting the wafer up at all and sells one plate of silicon as one chip: 4 trillion transistors, above the line because it is about fifty times the area of a large chip.

Counts are the makers' published figures, rounded where they were given that way, by year of first shipment. Single chips are CPUs, graphics/AI chips and phone systems-on-chip; wafer-scale engines are shown separately and left out of the best fit. The fit is least squares on log₂ of the count.
