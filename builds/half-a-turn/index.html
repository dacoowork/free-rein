How it works · the click pen

Half a Turn

Click a ballpoint once and the tip stays out. Click it again and it goes back in. There is no switch inside, only a plastic ring that turns about 45° every time you press. Push the plunger and watch it go round.

Drag the plunger down in the cutaway (or anywhere on the unrolled wall), press Click, or tap the space bar. The red lugs belong to the cam, the ring that turns.

  1. 1PushThe plunger's teeth drive the cam down. Its lugs slide down the track between the ribs.
  2. 2NudgeBelow the ribs the lugs are free, and the plunger's slanted teeth shove them sideways.
  3. 3CatchLet go and the spring pushes up. Each lug hits a sloped rib end and slides into its notch: tip out.
  4. 4Drop inNext time the nudge lands it on the other slope, which leads into the open track. Up it goes: tip in.

Things to try

Half a press

Push only part of the way and let go. The lugs never clear the rib ends, so nothing can turn them, and they come back to where they were.

Hold it down

The tip pokes out further than it rests. The cam has already turned at the bottom of the press; the catch only happens on the way up.

Eight clicks

Four ribs and eight plunger teeth round the inside mean eight clicks carry the cam one full turn and every lug back to its starting track.

Cam

Why it's built like this

No springs or levers to latch

The tip is held out by a lug sitting in a notch, pressed there by the same spring that wants to pull the tip back in. One spring does both jobs, and the only moving parts are two plastic rings.

It always turns the same way

Every slope in the barrel and on the plunger leans the same direction, so the cam can only ever go round one way. Nothing has to remember whether the tip is in or out: the cam's angle is the memory.

Notch, track, notch, track

Each rib has two sloped ends. The left one ends in a notch, the right one runs into the open track. A nudge of half a rib's spacing means the lugs land on alternate slopes, which is why clicks alternate.

The refill turns too

On pens where the refill rides on the cam, the refill turns with it. Parker advertised exactly that on its 1954 Jotter: a little turn every click, so the ball would wear evenly.

The drawings are a working model: every position of the cam comes from where its lugs touch the ribs and the plunger's teeth, with one spring pushing up. Proportions are stretched so the parts can be seen.
