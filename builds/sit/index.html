1

Squat

Before chairs, and still today

For most of human history sitting down meant going down. The deep squat, heels flat and bottom near the ground, is still an ordinary resting posture in many parts of the world, for waiting, talking and eating. It needs no furniture at all.

Look at the readout: the knee folds almost shut. If you can no longer do this comfortably, you're in a very large club.

No seat, no back, no legs to bump.
2

Stool

Ancient Egypt and onwards

The first furniture to lift you was a seat with nothing behind it. Egyptian tombs hold stools, many of them folding, and Tutankhamun's held several. A stool puts the knees at a right angle and lets you lean forward over work.

It also leaves your back to look after itself, which is why the stool has never gone away: bar, piano, milking pail.

Knees near 90°. Back: your problem.
3

Throne

Greece, Rome, medieval Europe

For centuries a chair with a back and arms meant rank. The bishop's seat was the cathedra, and that is where the word cathedral comes from. We still say a meeting has a chair, and a professor holds one.

Notice the footstool. A high seat left the feet dangling, so rulers put a block under them, and the whole figure sits above everyone else's heads.

Upright, raised, and not allowed to fidget.
4

Bentwood

Vienna, 1859

Michael Thonet steamed beech rods and bent them into curves. His No. 14 chair was famously built from six pieces of wood, ten screws and two nuts, and it shipped as a flat kit. Millions were sold, and the café chair was born.

It was the first seat the ordinary public could buy by the dozen. The posture is the plain upright one, with the back lightly supported.

Six pieces, ten screws, two nuts.
5

Swivel

Offices, from the 19th century

Wheels and a spring let a chair follow the worker. Charles Darwin is said to have fitted castors to his study chair to roll between his specimens. Later office chairs added tilt, and measurements of spinal discs since the 1960s found that leaning back eases the load on them.

So the hip angle opens past a right angle, and the keyboard comes to you.

Hip angle opens up. The desk does the rest.
6

Kneeler

Norway, 1979

Peter Opsvik's Balans chair tipped the seat forward and gave the shins a pad to rest on. The idea was that the pelvis rolls forward, the hips open and the spine stacks up naturally.

The cost is pressure on the shins and nowhere to put your legs when you want out. Plenty of people loved it, and plenty gave theirs to the attic.

Hips wide open. Shins not thrilled.
7

Stand

The 2010s desk

When sitting all day became a worry, desks learned to rise. The knee straightens, the hip opens fully, and the readouts hit their limit. Yet most ergonomic advice does not say "stand instead". It says change position often, because any single posture tires you out.
