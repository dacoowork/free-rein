Late Plastic

Ten thousand years from now, a team digs through a rubbish tip sealed under clay in what used to be the English Midlands. Everything in it was thrown away between about 1950 and 2100, the age its finders call the Late Plastic.

Pick a numbered find in the trench, brush the soil off it in the tray, and it goes into the hall with the curators' label. Turn the label over for what the thing really was, and why it lasted.

All ten finds are recorded. The curators got the bird right and the mug wrong, which is about the usual rate. Of the ten, the mug and the wishbone have the best chance of lasting long enough to turn to stone; the plastics will probably outlive everyone who used them, as fragments, as stains in the clay, and as a thin layer that geologists of any age will be able to find.

The finds are imagined; the materials, dates and survival notes are real. Toy-brick lifetimes come from a 2020 University of Plymouth study; the chicken as a marker of our age from Bennett and colleagues, 2018.
