Why does a queue go from fine to hopeless when the shop gets only a little busier? Three experiments with supermarket tills, run on simulated shoppers who turn up at random and take a minute each, on average, to serve.

Experiment 1 · One till

A little busier, a lot longer

Till 7 can serve 60 shoppers an hour. Slide the number of shoppers arriving each hour and watch the line. At half capacity nobody waits long. Push the till to 90% busy and the average wait is nine minutes, even though the till still has spare time six minutes in every hour.

Experiment 2 · The hockey stick

The curve that bends upward

For one till, the average wait depends on just two things: how busy the till is, written ρ, and how much basket sizes vary. The Pollaczek–Khinchine formula puts them together:

wait = ρ ⁄ (1 − ρ) × (1 + c²) ⁄ 2 × service time

The first part is what makes the hockey stick. As ρ heads for 1, 1 − ρ heads for zero and the wait has no ceiling. The second part, with c the spread of basket sizes, scales the whole curve. If every basket took exactly a minute, waits would halve. Mix in weekly shops behind people buying a sandwich and they go up instead. Pick a mix here and it also changes the shoppers at till 7.

Experiment 3 · Four tills

One line or four?

The same shoppers, with the same baskets, arrive at two identical rows of four tills. On the left each shopper joins the shortest line and stays in it. On the right everyone waits in one snake and goes to the next free till. The snake is faster on average, and nobody watches a later arrival get served first.

Shoppers arrive at random (a Poisson stream), so gaps between them are sometimes long and sometimes very short, and that randomness is the whole reason queues form below 100% busy. Service averages one minute. In “normal mix” service times are exponentially spread (c = 1); “wildly mixed” is a blend of quick shops and big trolleys with c² = 3.
