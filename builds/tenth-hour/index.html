Tenth Hour

In 1793 the French Republic decimalised time. The day was cut into ten hours of a hundred minutes of a hundred seconds, and the year into twelve months of thirty days, with a ten-day week. Here is that clock running now, and that calendar for any date you like.

The year

The year began on the day of the autumn equinox at the Paris Observatory. Months were named by the poet Fabre d’Églantine for the weather and work of each season. Tap a month to jump to its first day.

Autumn Vendémiaire, vintage · Brumaire, mist · Frimaire, frost

Winter Nivôse, snow · Pluviôse, rain · Ventôse, wind

Spring Germinal, seed · Floréal, blossom · Prairial, meadow

Summer Messidor, harvest · Thermidor, heat · Fructidor, fruit

Sansculottides Five feast days to close the year, six in a leap year (a sextile year)

Decimal clocks were made official in 1794 and suspended the next spring; hardly anyone bought one. The calendar lasted longer, until Napoleon ended it after 10 Nivôse XIV, the last day of 1805. The Paris Commune used it again for a few weeks in 1871.

Year starts here come from the equinox, computed for each year and taken in Paris local time, as the 1793 decree specified. Decimal time is counted from your own midnight.
