One line isn't enough

From a tower you can tell exactly which direction the smoke is in, but not how far away. A plume eight kilometres off looks much like one at three, and the base is usually hidden behind a nearer ridge. A bearing gives you a line on the map; the fire is somewhere on it.

Two lines cross

A second tower, looking from somewhere else, draws a second line. The fire is where they cross. The cross is sharpest when the lines meet near a right angle; when two towers look along almost the same line, a half-degree of error slides the crossing a long way.

Three make a hat

Three real bearings almost never meet at a point. They make a small triangle, the cocked hat, and its size is an honest measure of the error. Lookouts in the American West used a sighting table called the Osborne Firefinder, devised in the 1910s, and radioed their readings to a dispatcher who drew the lines on a big map.

Hollin Forest, its towers and its fires are invented. The views are drawn from the same terrain as the map, so every ridge you see from a tower is a real ridge on the contours.
