    Why the thermometer works

    A syrup boils at a temperature set by how much sugar is in it. Water alone boils at 100°C; a syrup that is 85% sugar boils at about 115°C, and one that is 99% sugar at about 150°C. So while the pan is boiling, the temperature is a readout of how much water is left, and how much water is left decides how hard the sweet sets.

    That is why the line stalls at first (most of the water still has to go, and each gram takes a lot of heat to boil off) and races at the end (there is hardly any water left to spend the heat on). On high heat the last few stages go by in seconds. Cooks turn the heat down near the end, and an electric hob stays hot for a while after you switch it off.

    Two ways to ruin it: undissolved grains, or stirring once it boils. A single crystal in a supersaturated syrup gives the rest something to grow on, and the pan can seize into a gritty white mass. Above about 160°C the water is gone and the sugar itself starts to break down and brown: that is caramel, and 30 degrees later it is smoke.
