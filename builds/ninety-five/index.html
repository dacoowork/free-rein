A short read · No. 95Stripes, shops & arithmetic

NINETY-FIVE

Every barcode on every packet in the shop is ninety-five thin columns, black or white. This is how a doodle in the sand became the stripes that run the tills, and how to read one with your own eyes.

Seven short sections. Everything drawn here is a real, scannable EAN-13 built live on this page.

1

8:01 in the morning, Troy, Ohio

On 26 June 1974, at the Marsh Supermarket in Troy, Ohio, a ten-pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit gum slid across a pane of glass at the checkout. A red laser underneath swept across the stripes printed on its side, the till beeped, and 67 cents appeared on the display. It was the first product ever sold by scanning a Universal Product Code.

The gum was chosen almost by accident: it was the first thing in the basket. The pack is now in the Smithsonian. What it carried was not a picture of a price or a name. It was a twelve-digit number, written twice: once in figures for people, and once in stripes for the machine.

The stripes don't say "gum". They say a number. The shop's computer looks the number up.

That idea, a number the machine can read and the shop can look up, had been waiting twenty-five years for cheap lasers and cheap computers to catch up with it.

2

Dots and dashes in the sand

In 1948 a graduate student at the Drexel Institute in Philadelphia, Bernard Silver, overheard a supermarket boss asking a dean whether the college could find a way to read product information automatically at the till. He told his friend Norman Joseph Woodland, who took the problem with him to Miami Beach.

Woodland knew Morse code from the Boy Scouts. Sitting on the beach, he pushed his fingers into the sand and drew dots and dashes. Then, without quite meaning to, he pulled them towards him. The dots became thin lines and the dashes became thick ones. Morse code, stretched, was a barcode.

3

The bullseye that smeared

Woodland and Silver filed a patent in 1949 and were granted it in 1952. Their favourite version was not stripes but a bullseye: rings of different widths, which could be read from any direction, so the cashier wouldn't need to turn the packet.

Nobody could build the reader cheaply for another twenty years. When the American grocery trade finally set up a committee to pick one symbol, RCA tried the bullseye in a Kroger store in Cincinnati in 1972. It had a problem that only showed up on a printing press: paper moves through the rollers in one direction, and ink smears a little in that direction. Smear a ring and it gets fatter at the top and bottom. Smear a stripe along its own length and nothing that matters changes.

4

Ninety-five modules

Look closely at a barcode and the stripes come in only four widths. That's because the whole thing is drawn on a grid of 95 equal columns called modules, each one either black or white. At full size a module is a third of a millimetre wide. A bar is one, two, three or four modules wide; so is a gap.

Each digit takes seven modules, always as two bars and two gaps. Six digits on the left, six on the right, and three fixed patterns: 101 at each end and 01010 in the middle. Those guards, which reach lower than the rest, show the scanner how wide one module is, so it can measure everything else against them. That's why the barcode can be printed big or small.

5

The digit that isn't there

The American code had twelve digits. In 1977 Europe adopted the same symbol but wanted thirteen, to leave room for country prefixes. The difficulty was that every scanner already in the world expected exactly 95 modules.

The trick was to hide the extra digit in a choice. Each left-hand digit can be drawn two ways: set L (an odd number of black modules) or set G (the right-hand pattern reversed, an even number). The order of L's and G's across the left six spells the hidden first digit. An American code is simply a European one that starts with 0, which uses L six times: exactly the old pattern, so nothing had to change.

6

The thirteenth digit checks the other twelve

The last digit carries no information. It's arithmetic. Multiply the twelve digits alternately by 1 and 3, add them up, and the check digit is whatever brings the total to the next multiple of ten. If the scanner misreads a single stripe and gets one digit wrong, the sum won't end in 0 and the till stays silent instead of charging you for the wrong thing.

It also catches most cases of two neighbouring digits swapping places, which is how people mistype numbers. The one swap it misses is two digits that differ by exactly 5, such as 1 and 6, because 3 × 5 and 1 × 5 leave the same last digit.

There's one more safety catch built into the stripes. Every left-hand digit in set L has an odd number of black modules, and every right-hand digit an even number. So a scanner reading the code upside down, right to left, notices straight away and flips it round. The cashier never has to.

7

Make one, then scan it

Type twelve digits and the page works out the thirteenth, chooses the L and G sets, lays out the 95 modules and draws them. Then send the laser across. The reader doesn't peek at the digits you typed: it measures the black and white runs it crosses, turns them back into modules, looks each group of seven up, and checks the sum.

Fifty years on, the stripes have company. QR codes and the newer two-dimensional codes on packets carry whole web addresses and batch numbers. But a beep at the till is still, nearly always, a laser measuring ninety-five columns and doing a sum.
